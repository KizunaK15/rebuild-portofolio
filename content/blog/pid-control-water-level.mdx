## Introduction

PID control is the backbone of most industrial process control systems. Despite its age, it remains the dominant algorithm in production because it is predictable, tunable without a model, and runs on a microcontroller with microseconds to spare. This article documents the exact process used to implement and tune a water level controller on Arduino Uno.

## The Plant

The system is a single-tank water level plant: a pump fills the tank, a drain valve creates a load disturbance, and a VL6180X Time-of-Flight LIDAR sensor measures level at 50 ms intervals. The LIDAR was chosen over ultrasonic sensors because its accuracy at short distances (5–100 mm) is dramatically better — standard HC-SR04 sensors have ±3 mm noise on a calm surface, the VL6180X delivers ±1 mm.

## System Identification

Before writing control code, spend time characterizing the plant. A step test — turning the pump fully on and recording the level response — yields the process gain, time constant, and dead time needed for initial PID parameters via Ziegler-Nichols or Cohen-Coon tuning rules.

```c
// Simple step test logger
void loop() {
  float level = readLidar(); // mm from sensor to water surface
  Serial.print(millis());
  Serial.print(",");
  Serial.println(level);
  delay(50);
}
```

## Tuning Results

After three iterations of manual fine-tuning from the Ziegler-Nichols starting point:

- Kp = 2.1, Ki = 0.08, Kd = 0.4
- Steady-state error: < 2.5 mm
- Overshoot: < 2% of full-scale range
- Settling time: < 8 seconds from a 10 cm step

The derivative term was implemented as derivative-on-measurement (not derivative-on-error) to eliminate derivative kick on setpoint changes.
